# Moral Hermeneutics and Technology: Making Moral Sense through Human-Technology-World Relations — Olya Kudina
**Format**: md | **Pages**: substantive text 1–140 | **Sections**: 5 | **Depth**: study

Source: Lexington Books, 2024. Five numbered chapters, with an introduction and substantial design/governance conclusion. This reference distills concepts and reasoning; it does not reproduce the book or its illustrations. References to technologies describe the source's study contexts, not verified current capabilities.

## Mental Model (read first)

Values are lived, interpreted, and sometimes reconfigured through encounters with technologies. People do not simply apply a finished moral vocabulary to an inert innovation: they try to make sense of it using prior experience, while the encounter can change what their values mean. Investigate that reciprocal process in concrete accounts of appropriation. Preserve the difference between understanding value change and justifying it.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 5. Hermeneutic Lemniscate as an Encompassing Principle of Moral Sense-Making Mediated by Technologies

- **Moral hermeneutics** concerns the interpretation and reinterpretation of moral meaning through human–technology–world relations. Ask how people identify what matters, how an artifact changes the situation, and how those experiences reshape their prior understanding. This exceeds a checklist of benefits and harms under fixed value labels.
- **Gadamer's hermeneutic circle.** Understanding draws on prior assumptions and revises them through encounter. Those assumptions are conditions of interpretation rather than simply errors to eliminate. Their productive role does not make every interpretation correct; show how an encounter supports, resists, or revises them.
- **Material hermeneutics.** Ihde's account gives technologies an active role in how a world is encountered. Kudina argues that an encompassing account must also explain the return movement: how the mediated encounter updates a person's embodied and cultural awareness. Preserve this as her extension and criticism, not a proof that earlier authors denied all reciprocity.
- **Hermeneutic lemniscate: human, technology, world, and return.** The infinity-shaped model names a unified sense-making process. A person appropriates an artifact through prior awareness; the artifact selectively mediates a sociocultural world; the resulting encounter reconstitutes the person's understanding and possibilities for further action. It is not two independent circles or a claim of equal influence among the three elements.
- **Body One and Body Two.** Bodily, sensorimotor experience and sociocultural awareness participate in interpretation. Inspect how a design assumes a particular body, speech pattern, language, or social convention. Exclusion from a supposedly seamless relation can disclose the assumptions of the “standard user.”
- **Temporary stabilization.** Meaning becomes sufficiently settled for action through the relation, yet remains open to further encounters. Do not infer constant radical flux from value dynamism or permanent settlement from a moment of agreement.
- **Voice-assistant illustration.** Command recognition, response style, and expectations about language can shape how a person speaks and what they expect from an interaction. Investigate both the device's selective responses and the person's subsequent adaptation. Claims about particular accents, disabilities, or present products need evidence beyond this historical illustration.
- **Normative feedback.** A question about courtesy, respect, or inclusion must follow how the encounter returns to future expectations and relations. A plausible transfer from device use to interpersonal behavior is a hypothesis until supported; the lemniscate makes the question visible without automatically proving the transfer.

### 1. Morality as an Ecosystem

- **Morality as an ecosystem.** Values coexist within sociomaterial practices, relating to and modifying one another. Changing a practice can alter which values become salient and how they are specified. Begin with a concrete problematic situation, not a list of supposedly independent values with stable weights.
- **Values-in-practices.** A general word such as privacy, health, or care does not specify its local meaning. Identify the relationships and activities it is meant to protect or enable. Abstract definitions remain useful starting points, but they must be interpreted through the actual situation.
- **Dewey's theory of valuation.** Shift from asking only what values are to examining how people form and assess them. Valuation involves consequences, reflection, and concrete activity. This avoids treating values either as detached final standards or merely private feelings without public reasons.
- **Ends-in-view.** Values can operate as provisional aims that guide action and are tested and revised through its consequences. They are not fixed finalities standing outside experience. Ask what proposed end directs the current practice, what means it requires, and what the attempt teaches participants about the end itself.
- **Relational value formation.** Technology, human history, and a social setting jointly shape the circumstances in which a value becomes meaningful. This does not attribute every normative change to one device. Trace the relevant relations and acknowledge other influences.
- **Stable guidance without immutability.** Moral commitments can become durable through repeated experience. Their history of practical adequacy can support their authority without establishing that they fit every new configuration. A difficult case should prompt interpretation and justification rather than automatic abandonment of the commitment.

### 2. Technological Mediation of Morality

- **Moral malleability and potentiality.** New interactions can disclose possibilities for revising settled expectations. Potentiality is not a predetermined end unfolding inside a person or technology; what becomes possible depends on the encounter. Avoid predicting a particular value change solely from a product's existence.
- **Thin and thick morality.** In Kudina's use of Walzer, richly situated moral understandings and thinner shared formulations belong together. Do not treat contextual morality merely as a local decoration added to a complete abstract system. Examine what a general term leaves out of the lived practice.
- **Technomoral change.** This approach investigates how technology and moral frameworks co-evolve, often with an eye to societal futures and governance. Kudina's moral mediation account supplies close attention to appropriation and sense-making. These perspectives can complement one another without being interchangeable in scale or evidential basis.
- **Macro-, meso-, and micro-level moral change.** The reviewed technomoral-change literature distinguishes broad principles, their specification within practices, and concrete questions of action. These are normative levels, not Ihde's microperception and macroperception. State which framework a term belongs to before comparing them.
- **Value change and value dynamism.** Investigate whether the case involves a revised conception, interpretation, practical specification, or another change in the way a value operates. A new application of “privacy” need not prove that privacy has been abandoned or replaced. State the particular change supported by the account.
- **Technology as a participant in interpretation.** A device contributes not only by adding options but by helping establish the terms under which options matter. Compare the person's understanding before, during, and after encounter, while recognizing that interviews reconstruct these moments rather than directly recovering an untouched past.

### 3. Technological Appropriation and Moral Hermeneutics

- **Technological appropriation.** People make an artifact meaningful in relation to their lives. This includes practical integration and projective sense-making before direct use. A participant can meaningfully interpret an emerging technology without having tried it, but that interpretation is not evidence of actual effects in use.
- **Domestication and mediation.** Domestication studies illuminate incorporation into daily routines and symbolic life. Kudina extends attention to the projective and moral dimensions of appropriation. Keep differences of emphasis visible rather than claiming that all domestication research ignores interpretation.
- **Projective appropriation.** When a technology is unfamiliar, people draw on past experience, narratives, promotional material, and social context to situate it. Those informational settings themselves shape the response. Record what participants were shown and what they already knew.
- **Google Glass and privacy.** The book analyzes public comments to explore how people anticipate recording, trust, social interaction, memory-making, and boundaries between public and private. The same general appeal to privacy can protect different practices. Treat the comments as situated anticipations, not a representative verdict by all users or by society.
- **Proof of principle.** The preliminary Glass analysis shows the usefulness of examining moral meaning in appropriation. Its role is exploratory. It motivates richer empirical investigation; it does not validate a universal theory of adoption or quantify the likelihood of a particular social outcome.

### 4. Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis as a Method to Study Moral Hermeneutics

- **Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA).** IPA combines phenomenology, hermeneutics, and idiography to examine how people make sense of significant experience. Kudina adapts it to empirical philosophy, asking what accounts of technological appropriation imply for moral meaning. It is not simply sentiment analysis or a frequency count of value words.
- **Double hermeneutics.** The researcher interprets a participant's interpretation. Make both layers visible. Audio recording, transcription, prompts, translation, and analytic categories can add mediation; a transcript is not direct access to an unprocessed experience.
- **Idiographic commitment.** Attend deeply to individual cases before comparing them. Convergence and divergence both matter. An unusual account may reveal an important issue even if it appears rarely. Do not convert a small qualitative sample into a population estimate.
- **Conversation Analysis and Discursive Psychology (CA&DP).** Kudina compares this approach's attention to interactional and discursive construction with IPA's suitability for interpreting experiential content and projective sense-making. Choose a method for the question being asked; this comparison is not a claim that one method is generally superior.
- **Practical study sequence.** Define a situated question; recruit participants with relevant experiences and a defensible scope; elicit rich accounts with open, semi-structured prompts; transcribe attentively; interpret each case; develop and relate themes; compare cases while retaining disagreements; connect the empirical interpretation back to the philosophical claim. This is a compact reconstruction, not a replacement for a full IPA methods manual.
- **Evidential traceability.** Show what material supports a theme, how it was obtained, and how interpretation proceeds. Separate a participant's claim, the researcher's reading, and the philosophical inference. Plausibility depends on that chain, not on the sophistication of the final conceptual vocabulary.
- **Emerging technology and lived experience.** Projective appropriation can be anchored in experiences of family, care, work, or social expectations even without firsthand use of the proposed device. Keep what is lived separate from what is imagined so the analysis does not silently convert anticipated consequences into observations.
- **Sex-selection technology (SST+) study.** Seven participants situated in Dutch culture discuss an emerging technology for non-medical sex selection. Their varied backgrounds support rich comparison rather than statistical representation. Family histories and views about parenthood inform their sense-making; the study illustrates a method, not the present legality, technical performance, or acceptability of sex selection.

### Conclusion: Design and Governance

- **Value Sensitive Design.** Conceptual, empirical, and technical inquiry can incorporate moral concerns into design. Kudina questions approaches that privilege a preselected value list and only later specify it in context. Use prior lists as prompts while remaining open to values and interpretations made visible by the practice itself.
- **Designing for evolving moral meaning.** A requirement meant to support a value should remain answerable to how that value is enacted in use. Revisit requirements when appropriation changes the situation, rather than treating the original specification as a final moral settlement.
- **Collingridge dilemma.** Early in development, consequences are difficult to know; once consequences become clearer, established systems may be hard to change. Moral hermeneutics can investigate emerging interpretations before broad uptake, while remaining modest about prediction.
- **Sociotechnical experiments.** The reviewed approach treats innovation as intervention with uncertain consequences and emphasizes responsible learning. Kudina seeks a modest, grounded capacity to look forward that supplements experimentation without pretending to foresee societal outcomes exhaustively.
- **Mediation, technomoral scenarios, and governance.** Close studies of current or projective appropriation can inform broader deliberation. State the bridge from an individual account to a design or policy proposal. Further evidence, plural participation, and revisability are needed before that proposal becomes justified governance.
- **Description versus justification.** Showing how people adapt a value does not establish that the adaptation is desirable, voluntary, inclusive, or fair. Criticism can emerge within the same dynamic moral world through competing interpretations, consequences, and affected people's experiences.

## Worked Example

**Appropriating Google Glass and interpreting privacy**, reconstructed from chapter 3. Begin with the material and promotional scenario: a wearable camera can be used in ordinary encounters, while other people may be uncertain whether recording occurs or where the recording travels. Commenters make sense of this through existing experiences of trust, surveillance, memory, and public interaction.

“Privacy” consequently does not have one operational meaning in every response. For one concern, what matters is whether another person can record without notice; for another, it is the appropriateness of recording in a shared social situation or the consequences of later circulation. The analyst identifies the practices each appeal protects and how the new configuration makes those interpretations salient.

The justified conclusion is that projective appropriation can reveal value dynamism and competing meanings. The comments cannot establish how all users would behave, whether everyone would eventually accept the device, or which privacy settlement is morally justified. Those are additional questions with additional evidence requirements.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- When a value word appears, ask what concrete relationship or practice gives it content here.
- When someone alleges value change, identify the before-and-after interpretation and the evidence for the change.
- When the technology is not yet used, classify accounts as projective appropriation and preserve that limit in conclusions.
- When analyzing interviews, retain participant meaning, researcher interpretation, and philosophical inference as distinguishable layers.
- When a minority case challenges a theme, examine what it reveals before discarding it as infrequent.
- When designing for values, test whether a requirement preserves the relevant practice as people appropriate the artifact.
- When extending findings to governance, explain the scale change and seek further perspectives rather than treating a small sample as a mandate.
- When adaptation occurs, evaluate it; do not confuse becoming normal with becoming right.

## Key Takeaways

1. Values acquire meaning in sociomaterial practices.
2. The hermeneutic lemniscate makes the return movement of interpretation explicit.
3. Appropriation can be projective as well as practical.
4. IPA supports detailed interpretation, not population prediction.
5. Value dynamism strengthens the need for reflective, revisable design and governance.
