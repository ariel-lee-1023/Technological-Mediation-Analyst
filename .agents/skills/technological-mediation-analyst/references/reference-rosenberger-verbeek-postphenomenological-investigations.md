# Postphenomenological Investigations: Essays on Human–Technology Relations — edited by Robert Rosenberger and Peter-Paul Verbeek
**Format**: md | **Pages**: substantive chapters 9–251 | **Sections**: 16 | **Depth**: study

Source: Lexington Books, 2015. This is an edited collection. Attribute a chapter's proposal to its author, and distinguish the field guide from extensions and critical interlocutors. Page spans in headings are chapter locators, not claims that every page was read.

## Mental Model (read first)

Use concrete human–technology relations to test and revise philosophical concepts. The field's vocabulary supports close description, but unusual bodies, infrastructures, and institutional settings can reveal its limits. The collection contains disagreements about metaphysics, bodily boundaries, political structures, and normative judgment. Do not turn those differences into a single doctrine endorsed by every contributor.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. A Field Guide to Postphenomenology — Rosenberger and Verbeek, pp. 9–41

- **Empirical philosophy.** Actual practices, first-person experience, and empirical studies are starting points for conceptual inquiry. The work is not simply applying a settled taxonomy to examples. Ask how the case changes what counts as perception, agency, knowledge, or moral judgment.
- **Embodiment, hermeneutic, alterity, and background relations.** Describe perceiving through an incorporated artifact, interpreting a technological presentation, interacting with a quasi-other, and inhabiting a technologically shaped setting. A complex device may support several relations. Keep their experienced organization explicit instead of fixing one category per product.
- **Relational ontology.** People and world acquire particular forms in their mediated relations. A representation can constitute something as a risk and its reader as a responsible decision-maker. This is stronger than adding an interface between independently fixed subjects and objects.
- **Cyborg relations and immersion relations.** Physical human–technology integration and responsive technological environments extend the basic typology. These are later proposals, not additional categories already specified in Ihde's 1990 fourfold. Describe why the basic distinction is insufficient before adding terminology.
- **Fusion, somatechnologies, and augmentation.** Fusion emphasizes blurred bodily boundaries; the field guide cites Dalibert's term **somatechnologies** in that connection. Augmentation concerns an added layer in experience, as with augmented reality. Distinguish that perceptual layering from bodily fusion and from immersion in a responsive environment.
- **Field of awareness.** Examine what occupies attention, what recedes, and how strongly the configuration holds. A device's withdrawal is only one aspect of experience. A transparent interface can coexist with an encompassing transformation of what the user attends to.
- **Field composition.** Technological mediation can reorganize the entire experiential field, as with engrossing cinema or microscope work. Identify both the content brought forward and the surrounding situation displaced. Do not reduce every attentional effect to the physical visibility of a screen or control.
- **Sedimentation.** Past experience settles into habitual ways of encountering and using a technology. Those habits contextualize present experience and make some relations easier to resume than others. Investigate learning histories and interruption recovery instead of assuming that users switch relations freely at will.
- **Multistability and dominant stability.** Several relations are possible, but some are more familiar and entrenched. A dominant interpretation can become difficult to recognize as one possibility among others. Variation is useful when it discloses both alternatives and the practical conditions needed to sustain them.
- **Methodological orientation.** Establish the concrete practice, describe how the technology mediates a relation, examine how people and world are co-shaped, and articulate the philosophical consequence. This reconstructs the field guide's commitments; it is not a universal fixed interview or coding protocol.
- **Case families.** Imaging, implantations, and cell-phone use demonstrate different relations and conceptual problems. Empirical findings cited in these cases belong to their study settings and dates; they do not establish present device performance or universal behavioral effects.

### 7. Four Dimensions of Technological Mediation — Asle H. Kiran, pp. 123–140

Kiran supplies four perspectives on the two-sided character of mediation. Apply them to one situated relation, rather than labeling a product with a single dimension.

| Dimension | Paired structure | Question that discriminates between accounts |
|---|---|---|
| Ontological/existential | Revealing–concealing | What world and kind of subject become available, and what recedes? |
| Epistemological | Magnification–reduction | What becomes knowable or salient through this presentation, and what information or context is attenuated? |
| Practical | Enabling–constraining | What actions are made possible, easy, difficult, or unavailable? |
| Ethical | Involving–alienating | What happens to people's participation in shaping a good life? |

- **Two-sidedness.** These pairs are not mechanically “good versus bad.” Revealing requires selectivity; a constraint can protect an important practice. Establish what is happening in each dimension, then give reasons for the normative judgment. There is no arithmetic requirement to find equal benefits and harms.
- **Normativity across dimensions.** Ethical consequences do not occur only in the fourth row. Selective knowledge, an altered world, and constrained action can all matter morally. The ethical dimension adds explicit attention to involvement in the good life rather than absorbing every other dimension.

### 2. Why Postphenomenology Needs a Metaphysics — Lenore Langsdorf, pp. 45–54

- **Implicit metaphysics.** Empirical inquiry already relies on assumptions about what exists and how things persist or change. Langsdorf argues for making these assumptions explicit, drawing on pragmatist and process-oriented thought. Avoid treating a refusal of foundational certainty as proof that no ontological commitments remain.
- **Event and process orientation.** Interactions and potential consequences can be more informative than descriptions of fixed substances. In an analysis of an evolving technical practice, ask what must persist for it to remain that practice and what changes constitute a new relation. This is Langsdorf's intervention, not an uncontested field requirement.

### 3. What Robotic Re-embodiment Reveals about Virtual Re-embodiment — Kirk M. Besmer, pp. 55–71

- **Re-embodiment.** Telepresence can produce a sense of being at a remote or virtual site while the biological body remains elsewhere. Examine the feedback loop and practical interaction that establish presence. A report of “being there” requires analysis of its structure and limits, not automatic acceptance as bodily relocation.
- **Critique of the extension thesis.** Besmer challenges a simple analogy between re-embodiment and extending an ordinary bodily tool. Compare robotic and virtual arrangements rather than treating them as interchangeable. Attend to what remains possible for the situated body and what depends on the remote system.

### 4. Thinking Technology with Merleau-Ponty — Aud Sissel Hoel and Annamaria Carusi, pp. 73–84

- **Later Merleau-Ponty and indirect ontology.** The authors turn to expressive and relational resources in his later work, where symbolisms and artifacts contribute to how a world becomes accessible. Do not restrict his relevance to the familiar early account of bodily tool incorporation.
- **The measuring body.** Hoel and Carusi name a perceptual, symbolic, and technologically distributed system that supplies standards for encountering the world while being shaped by what it encounters. **Flesh**, **in-each-otherness (Ineinander)**, and the **circuit** emphasize this reciprocal formation. Measurement here exceeds numerical recording: tools and symbolisms help institute a style of seeing and knowing.
- **Instruments as productive of intelligibility.** Investigate how an instrument changes the relation in which something can be expressed and known. “An extension of the senses” can be too weak a description. The chapter is a conceptual resource for mediation, not evidence about the accuracy of a particular instrument.

### 5. Movies and Bodies — Marie-Christine Nizzi, pp. 85–104

- **Self-multistability versus multiple realizability.** Multiple realizability asks whether the same mental state could occur in different physical systems. Nizzi's question concerns variations in what the embodied self becomes or is understood to be. Do not answer one question with terminology drawn from the other.
- **Science-fiction variations.** Films such as *Avatar*, *Limitless*, *Elysium*, *Blade Runner*, and *Surrogates*, together with further fictional transformations, expose assumptions about enhancement, boundaries, embodiment, and selfhood. Treat fiction as a structured exploration of intuitions, not demonstrated technology or a forecast.
- **Body as frontier; imagined disembodiment.** Compare cases where an altered body expands possibilities with cases that change the meaning of being a self. Preserve the distinction between a reader's or viewer's response and a conclusion about actual persons with prostheses or disabilities.

### 6. Bodies as Technology — Fernando Secomandi, pp. 105–122

- **Technique versus technology.** A hard separation between bodily skill and material technology can hide the technical formation of the body itself. Secomandi challenges that separation and asks how human technique fits postphenomenology. Do not assume an untrained body standing outside technological relations.
- **Bodily skill and self-practice.** Learned gestures and routines are conditions of apparently natural use. Study how a body becomes capable of participating, including habituation and practices that shape subjectivity. The argument complicates the framework; it does not collapse a living body into an interchangeable external implement.

### 8. Tracing the Tracker — Yoni Van Den Eede, pp. 143–157

- **Self-tracking as mediated self-relation.** The person is both involved in measuring and encountered through the resulting measurements. The tracker helps constitute the object called “my performance,” rather than merely revealing a pre-existing transparent self. Examine which aspects of life become trackable and authoritative.
- **Data double.** A quantified presentation requires translation and interpretation. Distinguish bodily experience, the constructed record, and the apparent trainer or coach that addresses the user through norms. These perspectives may conflict without one simply being an unmediated original.
- **Background and cultural conditions.** Sensors, platforms, lifestyle norms, and data infrastructures belong in the account. A projected “exoself” is discussed speculatively; do not report it as an established empirical finding or equate the data double with the whole person.

### 9. A Century on Speed — Søren Riis, pp. 159–174

- **Movement and mobility.** Increased speed is a way of reorganizing human–world relations, not only a reduction in travel duration. Ask how changing mobility affects the setting and activities in which a person can participate.
- **Imaginative and practical multistability.** Riis discusses Whyte's distinction between perceptual possibilities and stable technologically mediated practices. A possible alternative interpretation does not by itself establish an alternative to historically organized mobility. Keep local variation in view while examining broader directional patterns.

### 10. Searching for Alterity — Frances Bottenberg, pp. 175–189

- **Humanoid-robot encounters.** Interviews with Bina48 reveal how participants work to establish, lose, and restore an interaction. Gaze, responsiveness, and the language of impairment or malfunction shape the encounter. Analyze both the machine's contribution and the human effort that sustains quasi-otherness.
- **Robo-etiquettes.** Emerging conventions concern how to interact with an unusual counterpart. An apparently relational response does not demonstrate machine consciousness, and disrupted conversation need not be explained solely as defective human social behavior. The chapter explores encounters and possibilities rather than certifying a robot's moral status.

### 11. Postphenomenology of the Robot Medical Student — Chris Kaposy, pp. 191–202

- **Dream of automation.** Turning ethical judgment into the mechanical application of a rule can make training seem efficient while undermining responsiveness to a person. The “robot” also diagnoses a style of human professional practice, not just literal automated equipment.
- **Clinical particularity.** A cultural crib sheet can replace attention with a stereotype. Inspect how information tools mediate the student's relation to the patient and the formation of ethical competence. The lesson is not to reject clinical information resources, but to examine how they organize judgment.

### 12. Mediating Multiplicity — Adam M. Rosenfeld, pp. 203–214

- **Brain-dead bodies and organ transplant protocols.** A body can be encountered as a deceased patient, a living non-patient donor body, and a source of organs for recipient patients. These are situated practical configurations with different concerns, not simply three verbal opinions about one unchanged clinical workflow.
- **Mediating multiplicities.** Protocols and organ procurement organizations coordinate transitions between these contexts. Examine whether each relation has appropriate time, space, and representation. This philosophical account does not establish current legal death criteria or authorize medical decisions.

### 13. Towards a Hermeneutics of Unveiling — Jan Kyrre Berg Friis, pp. 215–226

- **Interpretive conditions.** Imaging must be understood through acquired expectations, context, and the conditions that make something visible as significant. An image's apparent clarity does not eliminate the possibility that something important goes unnoticed.
- **Experience and empirical correction.** Reflection on experience alone may miss influences on interpretation and action. Friis calls for engagement with empirical and experimental findings. His stronger general claims about human conduct should remain attributed arguments rather than universal psychological laws.

### 14. Making the Gestalt Switch — Andrew Feenberg, pp. 229–236

- **Reification.** Technical rationality can organize people and social conditions as manageable objects. A close description of an individual interface may fail to show this organization. Shift attention toward how objects and systems are socially constructed and who can alter that construction.
- **Instrumentalization theory; dereification.** Feenberg seeks a more fluid relation between rational disciplines, systems, artifacts, and the demands of lived experience. Material performance and cultural meaning must be understood together. Institutional power and collective possibilities require more than a claim that users can appropriate artifacts differently.
- **Critical challenge.** Pluricultural interaction may create openings, but the route from that possibility to democratic change needs explanation. Name the institutions, decisions, and affected people that connect a local mediation to a political claim.

### 15. Postphenomenology with an Eye to the Future — Diane P. Michelfelder, pp. 237–245

- **Conceptual development and design anticipation.** New technologies can reveal limitations in the analytical vocabulary. For example, augmented displays combine looking through and looking at an interface, potentially creating conflict between relations. Such analysis can motivate a testable design concern without establishing an outcome in advance.
- **Future-oriented scope.** Postphenomenology must consider how its resources bear on emerging practices, not only refine labels retrospectively. Do not mistake a plausible scenario for a successful empirical evaluation or presume that embodiment guarantees attentional compatibility.

### 16. Stability, Instability, and Phenomenology — Albert Borgmann, pp. 247–251

- **Apparent continuity of vision.** “Powers of ten” presentations can smooth over transitions from ordinary embodied seeing to reading instruments and rendered data. Reconstruct those transitions instead of treating a seamless animation as one homogeneous view.
- **Normative challenge to variation.** Showing the many ways things can appear leaves open which things should matter decisively and how people learn to attend to them. Borgmann presses the field toward questions of significance beyond identifying multiplicity. Do not report this as the editors' resolution of the dispute.

## Worked Example

**Cell-phone use while driving**, reconstructed from the field guide, shows why transparency alone is insufficient. A practiced driver may embody the car, and a familiar phone may be easy to operate. During a compelling conversation, however, the distant interlocutor can occupy the foreground while the immediate driving situation recedes. The interface's ease does not establish compatibility between the two activities.

Field composition names the reorganization; sedimentation explains why a familiar phone relation is readily taken up and maintained. Compare specific tasks, modes of communication, and circumstances before generalizing. This is a philosophical reconstruction of an attentional mechanism, not a substitute for present safety evidence or a numerical estimate of crash risk.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- When a basic relation label leaves out the experience, inspect field composition, sedimentation, and the organization of the setting.
- When considering alternatives, distinguish imaginative possibility from sustainable practical multistability.
- When assessing a mediation, use Kiran's pairs to identify selectivity before assigning moral valence.
- When a case concerns an institution, add Feenberg's questions of objectification and the power to redesign.
- When a case concerns good life or attention, include Borgmann's challenge: variation alone does not settle significance.
- When describing robots or cyborgs, separate experienced quasi-otherness, bodily integration, conscious agency, and moral responsibility.
- When citing this collection, identify the chapter author. Preserve objections rather than averaging them into consensus.

## Key Takeaways

1. Empirical cases can change the concepts used to analyze them.
2. Transparency, field composition, and sedimentation describe different features.
3. Mediation has interconnected ontological, epistemological, practical, and ethical dimensions.
4. Local appropriation does not exhaust institutional power or normative evaluation.
5. The collection is a debate, not a single unified position.
