## Mental Model (read first)

Technologies participate in moral life by shaping actions, interpretations, responsibilities, and the subjects who deliberate. This does not make every artifact a conscious moral agent or make criticism impossible. Ethics can engage these relations from within, helping people and designers shape them responsibly. Designing for morality requires anticipation, judgment, participation, and revision because actual mediation emerges in use.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Mediated Morality, p. 1

- **Moral mediation.** Artifacts help organize both morally significant conduct and the ways a situation becomes a moral question. A speed bump influences driving; ultrasound shapes what parents encounter and must decide. An adequate ethical analysis includes both behavior and the frameworks of interpretation behind it.
- **Experience and praxis.** Technologies mediate how the world appears and how people act within it. A representation can change action without explicitly commanding it. Look for the relation between selective presentation, perceived responsibilities, available actions, and actual conduct.
- **Human–technology relations and multistability.** Incorporation, reading, interaction, and background conditions provide different routes of mediation. The material configuration matters, but the eventual role also depends on use and appropriation. A design brief therefore cannot establish the artifact's complete moral significance.
- **Beyond instrumentalism and determinism.** Neither a neutral tool wielded by an autonomous chooser nor a machine that fully determines behavior captures the relation. Identify the combined practice and the room people have to interpret, negotiate, resist, or modify it.

### 5. Morality in Design, p. 90

- **Design as material ethics.** Design choices help organize how people will act and experience the world. Ethical responsibility extends beyond defects, safety, or malicious intent to the mediating role of an ordinarily functioning product. Assess explicit behavioral steering and implicit moral effects.
- **Sustainable technology and rebound effects.** Efficient equipment can invite more intensive use, avoidance, or workarounds. Evaluate the relation between artifact and behavior, not only the efficiency of the component. The book's energy and waste examples illustrate a mechanism; their aggregate empirical claims are not present-day measurements.
- **Scripts, inscriptions, and appropriation.** A script is not simply the designer's intention stored in matter. Its actual form emerges from design, material possibilities, user interpretation, and context. Artifacts may delegate work to users, and effects can occur without deliberate inscription.
- **Anticipating mediations.** Connect design and use through informed imagination, comparison with existing practices, scenarios, simulations, prototypes, and engagement with stakeholders. These methods help disclose plausible relations; multistability prevents a guarantee that the intended conduct will follow.
- **Constructive Technology Assessment (CTA).** Bring development and its selection environment into contact. Verbeek augments stakeholder involvement with attention to how a technology will mediate participants' practices and interpretations. Asking people whether they want a feature is narrower than examining how it changes their situation.
- **Script logic and user logic.** Following Jelsma, distinguish the behavior invited by a configuration from how people interpret and appropriate it. Investigate both. A technically coherent script can fail because the practical logic of use differs, and use can introduce ethically important effects absent from the brief.
- **Mediation and Value Sensitive Design (VSD).** Conceptual, empirical, and technical investigations can be enriched by an account of changing practices and subjectivity. Analyze the values at issue, how they become concrete requirements, and how the resulting technology changes the conditions under which those values are understood.
- **Metamediation.** Intended mediations themselves have further mediating effects. A system designed to improve driving behavior may affect decisions about when to drive. Examine the practice that grows around an intervention rather than evaluating only the immediate target behavior.

**Four loci of moral assessment**, from the chapter's augmentation of stakeholder analysis:

| Locus | What must be assessed |
|---|---|
| Intended mediations | The explicit changes designers seek in conduct and interpretation |
| Implicit mediations | Unintended but anticipatable effects of the configuration |
| Forms of mediation | Whether forcing, persuading, or seducing is proportionate and acceptable in context |
| Eventual outcomes | What actually occurs after use, including appropriation and effects beyond the original target |

- **Responsibility, freedom, democracy.** These are also transformed by mediation. Identify how responsibilities move, what forms of freedom remain or emerge, and how people participate in shaping the rules embodied in a design. Their mediated character is a reason for examination, not permission to disregard them.
- **Five-step design integration.** Decide whether to deliberately moralize the design or examine its implicit mediation; articulate values and possible material embodiments; anticipate script logic, user logic, and metamediation; assess the four loci with stakeholders and attention to subjectivity; choose a design provisionally and revise in light of actual use. This reconstructs the chapter's sequence, not a prediction algorithm.
- **Eternally Yours.** Technical, economic, and psychological product lifetimes can diverge. Materials that age well, accessible repair, and engaging operation can encourage attachment. A design hypothesis about attachment must still be evaluated in use and in relation to its wider environmental effects.

### 4. Technology and the Moral Subject, p. 66

- **Subject constitution.** A technology does not only act on an already completed moral subject; it helps form the person who perceives a choice, accepts responsibility, and evaluates conduct. Ask what kind of parent, patient, consumer, or citizen the configuration invites someone to become.
- **Foucault: power and freedom.** A subject formed within relations of power is not thereby incapable of ethics. Freedom can be exercised in a reflective relation to those forces. Seek ways people can critically shape their technologically mediated lives rather than imagining that removing all influence reveals pure autonomy.
- **Moral codes, behavior, and ethics.** A rule, observed compliance, and a person's work on their own conduct are distinct. A device can change behavior without cultivating the same ethical relation in every user. Assess what a practice asks someone to attend to and become, not only whether a target action occurs.
- **Ethical substance.** What aspect of oneself becomes the object of moral work? In Verbeek's extension, technologically mediated subjectivity itself becomes a focus: one's relation to the practices, dispositions, and choices an artifact helps produce.
- **Mode of subjection.** Why and how does someone relate to an obligation? Explicit versus implicit mediation and forcing, persuasion, or seduction organize different relations to a norm. Avoid treating all influence as equivalent merely because each changes behavior.
- **Self-practices.** Deliberate ways of using and designing technologies can shape one's relation to their influence. Reflection, experimentation, and modified use are forms of ethical activity. The proposal is active appropriation, not a requirement to renounce technologies or perform arbitrary self-denial.
- **Teleology.** Identify the mode of existence or kind of subject the practice aims toward. A design that optimizes an observable behavior may cultivate a different way of living from the one stakeholders sought. Bring that difference into deliberation.

### 2. A Nonhumanist Ethics of Technology, p. 21

- **Humanist subject–object separation.** Conventional pictures of a free subject judging inert objects conceal the material organization of moral life. Verbeek challenges that separation without replacing ethics with worship of technology or denying the significance of people.
- **Obstetric ultrasound.** A sonogram does not merely add information. It presents the fetus in particular ways, reorganizes relations with expectant parents, and turns aspects of pregnancy into occasions for choice. Declining a test can also become a decision for which people feel responsible.
- **Nonhumanist/amodern ethics.** Include the material conditions of moral activity while retaining reflection and accountability. “Nonhumanist” here does not mean hostility to human welfare; it challenges the assumption that morality is exclusively accomplished by independent human subjects.
- **Sloterdijk's challenge.** The discussion of humanism and its formative media broadens attention from texts to technological environments. Keep the distinctions among cultivating humanity, material mediation, and speculative biological transformation. The chapter is not an endorsement of every intervention called posthumanist.

### 3. Do Artifacts Have Morality?, p. 41

- **Moral significance versus a full moral agent.** Something can contribute to moral action without consciousness, free will, or blameworthiness of the kind ascribed to a person. The claim concerns mediated agency. State explicitly which sense of “agency” a question requires before answering it.
- **Hybrid agency.** An action can be attributable in its accomplishment to a human–artifact association. The person-with-a-gun example challenges both purely human and purely object-centered explanations. Distributed efficacy does not itself resolve responsibility among designers, institutions, and users.
- **Intentionality without animism.** A technology can organize a direction of action or perception without entertaining an intention in a mind. Describe the material and relational basis of the direction; avoid moving from a useful philosophical term to a claim about machine sentience.
- **Freedom and moral action.** The fact that action is mediated does not automatically make it amoral or unfree. Analyze what space exists for judgment and self-formation in the arrangement. Conversely, recognition that all action is situated does not make coercive design morally harmless.
- **Ethics of the good life.** Asking how to live makes the material constitution of life visible in ways that an isolated choice model can miss. This broadens the field of moral reflection without demonstrating that consequence- or duty-based arguments are always irrelevant.

### 6. Moral Environments: An Application, p. 120

- **Ambient Intelligence.** Responsive environments can shape conduct from the background through sensing, interpretation, and adjustment. Examine both their barely noticed operation and the models of users they enact. The book's predictions about particular systems remain historical.
- **Persuasive Technology.** Feedback and tailored interaction may invite changes in conduct and attitudes. Assess the purpose, means, and effects, including implicit mediation beyond the intended persuasion. Successful behavior change is not a sufficient moral justification.
- **Persuasion, forcing, and seduction.** Reason-responsive feedback differs from physical constraint and from noncognitive attraction. The distinction clarifies how an intervention works and how people can relate to it. Actual designs can combine these modes; classification does not settle legitimacy.
- **Moral laziness and technocracy objections.** Taking over a task can displace reflection, yet material support can also sustain responsible action. Ask what judgment is being offloaded, what competence remains, whose values govern the environment, and how affected people can contest or reshape it.
- **Ethics of use as well as design.** Users can develop self-practices within an environment, while designers and institutions shape the available room for such practices. Avoid assuming that a nominal ability to refuse an influence establishes a practical ability to do so.

### 7. Morality beyond Mediation, p. 139

- **Cyborg intentionality: `(human / technology) → world`.** Verbeek distinguishes physical integration that forms a hybrid from ordinary use of an embodied artifact. The distinction concerns how the relation is constituted; it does not imply that a person with an implant is less worthy or less human in moral standing.
- **Composite intentionality: `human → (technology → world)`.** Human directedness engages the technology's own selective access to or construction of a world. Analyze the technological operation inside the presentation instead of treating the relation between device and world as an unexplained dash.
- **Augmented intentionality.** Extended exposures, for example, disclose a temporal presentation beyond ordinary human seeing. Ask which features are retained through the instrument's operation and which transient features disappear.
- **Constructive intentionality.** Technical arrangements can produce a perceptual presentation with no equivalent original in ordinary experience, as in the stereographic artworks discussed in the chapter. Distinguish revealing otherwise inaccessible features from constructing a novel experienced configuration.
- **Composite morality.** Technological interpretation and human evaluation can jointly organize moral action. This extends the analysis of mediation; it is not a claim that a system can independently supply legitimate moral conclusions.

### 8. Conclusion: Accompanying Technology, p. 153

- **Beyond the empirical and ethical turns.** Empirical attention can lose normative force, while applied ethics can forget that its values are themselves technologically mediated. Verbeek's proposed further turn joins scrutiny of actual relations with normative reflection on how to shape them.
- **Accompaniment.** Ethics should engage the formation and use of technologies rather than function only as an external boundary guard. Participation does not mean accepting every development: it makes the relations open to responsible examination and change.
- **Critique from within.** Moral frameworks being situated does not entail that criticism is impossible. Make the configurations and forms of subjectivity explicit, assess them with affected people, and retain the possibility of revision or rejection for articulated reasons.

## Worked Example

**Obstetric ultrasound**, reconstructed from the preface and chapter 2, changes the situation of expectant parents. Reflected sound is translated into an image that requires interpretation. A visible fetus can be encountered as a child with whom a relationship is developing and as a potential patient whose condition calls for further decisions. The mother and parents also acquire new roles within that presentation and its clinical setting.

Verbeek's account of seeking a limited scan illustrates that refusing additional information does not necessarily remove the felt responsibility to decide. The availability of tests already helps structure the choice. An artwork in the examination room provides another interpretive setting for thinking about disability and the child's life. The example shows that material, visual, institutional, and interpersonal elements jointly mediate moral meaning; it does not prescribe a reproductive decision or establish current clinical guidance.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- When asking whether an artifact is moral, distinguish moral significance, contribution to action, conscious deliberation, and responsibility.
- When evaluating a design, examine intended mediation, implicit mediation, form of influence, and actual outcomes separately.
- When predicting an effect, include appropriation, rebound, and metamediation; treat implementation as revisable.
- When an intervention changes behavior, ask what kind of subject and relation to obligation it also creates.
- When freedom or responsibility seems to disappear, investigate how it is reorganized and what capacities for reflection and modification remain.
- When ethics is framed as either endorsing or blocking technology, consider forms of participation that can reshape the relation itself.
- When applying medical or historical examples, extract the philosophical mechanism and independently verify any present practical facts.

## Key Takeaways

1. Technologies help constitute moral questions and moral subjects.
2. Material moral agency is not equivalent to conscious personhood.
3. Design requires anticipation, participatory assessment, and revision.
4. People can work on their mediated subjectivity from within technological relations.
5. Ethics can accompany technologies while retaining critical judgment.
