## Mental Model (read first)

Begin with what artifacts do in people's lives. Technologies help constitute how a world becomes meaningful and how people act within it; their significance exceeds both useful function and symbolic expression. Work from concrete relations toward philosophical judgments and design implications. Retain the critical force of earlier philosophy without substituting a theory of technological conditions for the actual activity of particular things.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 3. Postphenomenology

- **Empirical turn.** Study actual technologies and practices to correct philosophical overgeneralization. The turn does not mean abandoning philosophical questions for descriptions of adoption or accepting the existing social order. Empirical cases must inform how concepts such as agency, subject, and world are understood.
- **Postphenomenology.** The object of analysis is the human–world relation, not an allegedly unmediated world recovered by a privileged method. Subjectivity and objectivity acquire specific forms within that relation. An account of mediation therefore asks what kind of person and what kind of world become possible together.
- **Intentionality.** Experience and action are directed toward something. **Technological intentionality** characterizes how an artifact gives direction to human involvement. It does not imply that an ordinary artifact has conscious intentions. Specify the material organization through which a direction is invited or constrained.
- **Mediation versus an intermediary.** An intermediary would leave a relation essentially unchanged while conveying something across it. A mediator transforms the relation. A telephone, for instance, creates a distinctive way of encountering a distant person; it does not simply remove distance from an otherwise identical conversation.
- **Multistability.** An artifact has different possible identities in different relations and contexts. Its material properties constrain what can happen, but designer intention does not exhaust its activity. Look at both designed use and appropriation before describing “the” effect of the thing.
- **Two dimensions: experience and existence/praxis.** Ask how reality becomes present to people and how people become present and active in reality. A perceptual analysis alone misses action; a behavior analysis alone misses interpretation. These are connected dimensions of one relation, not separate causal channels sealed off from one another.

### 4. A Material Hermeneutic

- **Ihde's human–technology relations.** Embodiment and hermeneutic relations describe perceiving through and reading technologies; alterity concerns engagement with a technological quasi-other; background concerns the shaping of an environment. Use the distinctions to characterize use, not to classify entire products permanently.
- **Material hermeneutics.** Artifacts help shape interpretation through sensory presentation and through broader frameworks of meaning. Inspect what the device renders accessible and why that presentation becomes intelligible in a specific cultural or scientific setting.
- **Beyond subject and object.** Mediation does more than modify information delivered to an unchanged subject about an unchanged object. It helps constitute both poles. A particular instrument can bring forth an object as measurable, actionable, or threatening while also constituting a person as observer, decision-maker, or responsible user.
- **Amplification and reduction.** Mediated perception selectively strengthens and weakens aspects of what is encountered. The telephone makes distant speech available while reducing other forms of presence. Identify the transformation before deciding whether it matters favorably or unfavorably for the practice.
- **Low- and high-contrast transformation.** Eyeglasses and spectrographic displays transform perception differently. A display's distance from ordinary sensory appearance is not by itself an objection to its evidential value. Explain the interpretive work that connects the presentation to the phenomenon.
- **Microperception and macroperception.** Sensory encounter and cultural interpretation are mutually implicated. The cultural organization of seeing is neither an optional story added afterward nor sufficient to explain away material constraints.
- **Science as technological hermeneutics.** Scientific instruments establish new relations to phenomena and require skilled interpretation. Account for how knowledge is materially produced without reducing it either to unmediated observation or arbitrary social agreement.

### 5. The Acts of Artifacts

- **Latour's amodern ontology.** Humans and nonhumans acquire roles in associations rather than first existing as independent social and technical realms. **Actants** can make a difference in an association without being human persons. The **collective** includes their relations. Analytical symmetry does not erase all distinctions between people and objects.
- **Programs of action and antiprograms.** Describe the course of activity being pursued and what obstructs it. A program can be altered by adding an artifact; opposition can reorganize as well. Keep the language of function and intention descriptive before assigning responsibility.
- **Translation.** Mediation changes a program of action; it is not mere execution of an unchanged intention. Ask how the combination changes both what a person can do and what they are drawn to do.
- **Composition.** Agency is accomplished through an association of actants. Identify the combined arrangement responsible for an action's possibility rather than locating all efficacy in either a human intention or a thing. This is an account of action, not an automatic rule for allocating legal blame.
- **Reversible black-boxing.** A functioning combination can appear as a single thing while its dependencies disappear from attention. Failure or investigation can reopen the box. Trace those dependencies when they are relevant to the question; a complete network inventory is not required for every analysis of experience.
- **Delegation and scripts.** Material arrangements can carry tasks, invitations, and prescriptions, as with a weighted hotel key. A script must be studied in use. Mediation may be unintended, and artifacts can also delegate tasks to humans; reducing scripts to what designers consciously inscribed reinstates a one-sided account.
- **Postphenomenology and actor-network theory.** Both challenge the subject–object split, but their focal questions differ. Postphenomenology describes the differentiated human–artifact–world relation; actor-network analysis follows associations more broadly. Translate concepts carefully rather than calling every association a perceptual mediation.
- **Mediation of action: invitation and inhibition.** Artifacts make certain actions attractive, convenient, difficult, or unavailable. Identify the concrete material arrangement and actual appropriation. A practical inclination is not an irresistible command, and a reported intention is not proof of an effect.

### 6. Devices and the Good Life

- **Borgmann's device paradigm.** A device makes a commodity readily available while the machinery and associated effort withdraw. Availability promises immediacy, ubiquity, safety, and ease. Ask whether a practice has been reduced to procuring an output and which forms of engagement are displaced.
- **Focal things and practices.** Some activities gather meaningful involvement with things, people, and surroundings. A focal practice sustains that involvement through regular dedication. Its value cannot be exhausted by naming a commodity that a device could supply more efficiently.
- **Two forms of engagement.** Verbeek distinguishes necessary effort from meaningful involvement. Losing the labor of fetching water does not by itself show loss of the good life. Conversely, eliminating effort within an intrinsically meaningful activity can remove what made participation valuable.
- **Engaging devices.** Technology can support rather than only erode engagement. Distinguish involvement with the artifact, its surrounding context, and what it makes available. A sophisticated musical instrument or a technologically enabled practice can be deeply involving; age and simplicity are poor proxies for meaning.
- **Critical use of Borgmann.** Preserve the question of the good life but test the proposed contrast against actual devices and practices. Verbeek rejects the inference that all modern convenience necessarily destroys meaningful engagement. Keep this disagreement visible when combining his work with Roholt's use of Borgmann.

### 7. Artifacts in Design

- **Beyond functions and signs.** Products do more than perform tasks or express identity and lifestyle. They mediate relations materially. Evaluate how someone handles, inhabits, maintains, and lives with an object as well as what it accomplishes and signifies.
- **Material aesthetics.** Aesthetics concerns sensory involvement broadly, including touch and bodily action, not only visible appearance or beauty. A polished visual interface can still organize a poor practical relation. Examine the experience of using the thing.
- **Aesthetics and ethics.** Designing sensory and practical relations also helps shape how people live. Ethical reflection therefore belongs within design activity, rather than appearing only as a later assessment of manufacturing harms or functional safety.
- **Eternally Yours; cultural durability.** Product longevity depends on more than technical survival. Economic and psychological lifetimes matter, including obsolescence, attachment, and changing tastes. Durable attachment can arise through ordinary use without requiring every possession to become an emotionally irreplaceable heirloom.
- **Transparent artifacts.** Here transparency means understandable, accessible functioning that permits repair and a return to ordinary use after breakdown. It differs from perceptual transparency in embodiment relations. A sealed, opaque product may sever the relation when it fails, even if a component could otherwise be repaired.
- **Engaging artifacts.** A product can remain salient enough to invite participation while still supporting the activity. It need not either disappear entirely into functionality or become an obstructive object of attention. Select participation that enriches use; do not add arbitrary difficulty in the name of involvement.

### 1–2. Philosophy Beyond Things: Jaspers and Heidegger

- **Jaspers: technology and the self.** Mass production, the apparatus, and mass existence raise questions about how people can become themselves within organized technical life. His later approach treats technology as limited means needing direction. Verbeek finds the neutrality assumption insufficient because technical means themselves help shape ends and experience.
- **Heidegger: revealing, enframing, standing-reserve.** Technology can disclose reality as orderable resource rather than as neutral material awaiting use. This exposes a substantive philosophical problem, but a diagnosis at the level of modern technology's essence cannot substitute for discriminating between actual technologies and possible alternatives.
- **Readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand.** Equipment ordinarily withdraws within practical involvement; malfunction can make it conspicuous as an object. Verbeek uses these distinctions to reopen concrete material relations rather than infer that things have no active role while they work.
- **The thing things.** Heidegger's later account of things draws attention to gathering and meaningful presence. Verbeek brings this concern into dialogue with technical artifacts instead of reserving significant presence for a favored set of traditional objects.
- **The Orphic temptation.** An exclusive backward search for technology's conditions of possibility loses sight of concrete artifacts. Avoid replacing what things do with a general account of their origin, worldview, or essence. The remedy is philosophical analysis from and for things, not rejection of historical or ontological inquiry.

## Worked Example

**The weighted hotel key**, reconstructed from chapter 5, exposes practical mediation. A manager wants departing guests to return their keys. An oral request or written notice can be ignored or misunderstood. Adding a bulky weight changes the guest's experience of keeping the key: carrying it becomes inconvenient, making return attractive.

The action is composed through manager, guest, key, weight, and hotel routines. The added weight translates the program; it does not merely communicate the same message more loudly. Yet the result remains contingent: a guest might detach it, and another participant can introduce an antiprogram. Analyze both what was designed and what actually happens. Calling the key an actant explains its contribution without attributing conscious understanding to it.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- When an account speaks only of function or symbolism, add an account of material mediation in use.
- When a grand theory makes unlike artifacts equivalent, return to their specific relations and practical differences.
- When attributing technological intentionality, identify a direction in experience or action; do not invent a mind inside the object.
- When a script fails, inspect appropriation and context before blaming users for deviating from a supposedly fixed function.
- When convenience removes effort, determine whether the removed activity was burdensome, meaningful, or both.
- When designing for durability, support recoverable functioning and attachment through use; aesthetic novelty alone does not establish longevity.
- When combining Latour, Ihde, and Borgmann, preserve their different questions: distributed action, mediated experience, and meaningful engagement.

## Key Takeaways

1. Things mediate how people experience and inhabit a world.
2. Perceptual and practical transformations belong in the same analysis.
3. Material agency does not require conscious agency.
4. Meaningful engagement and mere effort are different.
5. Design can support attachment, repair, and richer forms of use.
